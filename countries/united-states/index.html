BUSINESS ECOSYSTEM

Business Ecosystem in the United States

Market context, strengths and the operating environment shaping the U.S. economy.

The United States is one of the world's largest and most diversified economies, with a nominal GDP of approximately $27.4 trillion in 2025, the highest globally (Source: World Bank). The country's open markets, strong rule of law, and innovation-driven culture support a mature and highly dynamic business environment across a broad range of sectors.

The U.S. hosts a deep mix of domestic leaders and multinational corporations. Over 33,000 foreign-owned companies operate in the country, employing more than 8 million Americans (Source: U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis). Major global businesses in technology, finance, healthcare, manufacturing, retail, and professional services maintain headquarters, regional hubs, or significant operations here, making the U.S. a central node in global commerce.

Key commercial strengths include a large consumer market, advanced infrastructure, world-class research institutions, access to capital, and a highly skilled workforce. The country leads in innovation, venture capital investment, and high-growth industries such as artificial intelligence, biotechnology, fintech, clean energy, aerospace, and advanced manufacturing.

The founder and startup environment is among the most active in the world. The U.S. attracted over $215 billion in venture capital investment in 2024, with hubs like Silicon Valley, New York City, Boston, and Austin driving entrepreneurship and scale-up growth (Source: PitchBook). A strong network of accelerators, angel investors, and corporate venture arms further supports early-stage and growth-stage companies.

Strategically located between North America and global markets, the United States plays a leading role in international trade, technology development, and global capital flows—reinforcing its position as a top destination for businesses, investors, and talent worldwide.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Find quick answers to the most common questions about doing business in the United States.

Yes, the United States offers one of the most business-friendly environments in the world. It has a large consumer market, strong legal protections, access to capital, advanced infrastructure, and a culture of innovation and entrepreneurship.

Most founders choose between an LLC (flexible, pass-through taxation) and a C-Corporation (preferred by venture-backed startups for equity and fundraising). Sole proprietorships and partnerships are common for smaller, owner-operated businesses.

Foreign nationals can legally form and own a U.S. company without a visa, but working in the business day-to-day typically requires an appropriate visa or status, such as E-2, L-1, or EB-5, depending on nationality and investment structure.

Costs vary by state and entity type, generally covering state filing fees, a registered agent, and optional legal or accounting support. Ongoing costs include annual state fees, franchise taxes in some states, and ordinary compliance filings.

C-Corporations pay a federal corporate tax rate of up to 21%, while LLCs and other pass-through entities generally report income on owners' personal tax returns. Businesses with employees also handle payroll tax withholding and reporting.

Non-residents typically need a registered U.S. business entity, an EIN (Employer Identification Number) from the IRS, and identity/business documentation. Some banks require an in-person visit, though a growing number of fintech and digital banks support remote account opening.

Technology & software, financial services, and healthcare & life sciences currently lead the U.S. economy in company count, founder activity, and market impact — see the Leading Industries section above for detail.

Startups can access angel investors, venture capital, accelerators and incubators, corporate venture arms, and R&D grants. The U.S. has the world's largest and most active VC market, along with a mature ecosystem of 5,300+ investors.
